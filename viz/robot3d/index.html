Built against a servo in hand, not a datasheet. Two things only a real part could say, both found by sliding a DS3225MG into a printed shoulder:

The cable leaves on the shaft's end. The slot was drawn at the end AWAY from the output shaft — the intuitive place, and wrong by 40 mm. The lead had nowhere to go, and the filler behind it was solid exactly where the wire wanted to be. It moved to the other end face, which cost nothing: the mounting ears sit 11.65 mm down the body and the slot sits at 29–39, so it passes 18 mm clear of the pads and their inserts. The filler's own relief opened from 10 × 10 to 16 × 16 at the same time — the bracket only has to let the lead through, but the filler is what it has to bend around, and the filler carries no load so the room is free.

A standard-size servo is not one size. Three listings for the same 25 kg class measure 40.0 × 20.5 × 40.5, 40.5 × 20.2 × 40.3 and 40.0 × 20.0 × 40.5, and the DS3225MG drawing gives 54.5 across the ears where this model said 54.0. Drawn to any one of them the pocket comes out 0.1 mm oversize for the others, which is less than the printer manages — the servo simply will not go in. The pockets now carry a fit allowance of their own, separate from the clearance used for rotating joints, and the housings grew with it so the wall around them stays at 3 mm. Every one of those three servos drops in with 0.7 mm or better.

Why a parallelogram. Crank and rocker are the same length and the rod equals the crank-axis-to-knee distance, so the rocker stays parallel to the crank and the servo reads the knee angle directly — ratio exactly 1.0 at every position. A general four-bar does not do this: of 45 geometries that covered the knee's range monotonically, the best fell to 0.12, which turns 11 kg·cm at the knee into 93 at a 25 kg·cm servo. The crank axis does not have to be the hip axis, only fixed to the thigh — that is what makes the exact version package.

What it buys. The knee servo moves off the knee and 46 mm up the thigh, cutting its share of the leg's swing inertia to a third.

No printed crank stops. A parallelogram has a crossed twin on the far side of a dead point, and the usual guard is a post the crank runs into — but the crank shares the shank's plane and the shank folds through it. The crank cannot reach a dead point under power: its travel stops 22.5° short at both ends, held there by the servo's range and the IK's joint limits.

The roll servo lies flat, and that one quarter turn fixed three separate-looking faults. A single 90° rotation put the servo's 40 mm length straight up, so every corner grew a 57 mm tower with the servo's bottom 4 mm sunk into the plate. The thigh grazed that tower at 26° of roll; the sunk pocket broke out through the plate's underside so you could see the filler from below; and only 1.0 cm³ of the four screw pads survived the clearance cuts. The quarter turn lays the body along the body's own Y instead — 26 mm tall, sitting ON the skin rather than in it. Roll goes back to ±35°, there is no floor to add and nothing to see, and the pads come back. HIP_Z was derived from the servo lying flat all along, so this is the geometry finally agreeing with the number the kinematics were already using.

The pitch horn had nothing to bolt to. Every journal here is a printed collar turning in a printed bore, and one module drew them all as a RING, bored to clear the servo horn. At the knee that is right, because the M4 pin passes through. At the hip it was not: slice the thigh and there was 0.000 cm³ of material inside the horn's screw circle anywhere between the plate and the servo's face. The horn hung 4 mm off the nearest plastic on four M3 screws spanning open air, carrying the whole leg in bending on the screws alone. It is a solid disc now.

And the journals were too short, which nothing was checking. A servo's output shaft is built for torque, not bending; the journal takes the bending instead, and the only thing that decides whether it can is how LONG it is. The thickness audit is a printability floor — a 4 mm collar with a 5.7 mm wall sails through it. Pressure goes as 6M/(D·L²), so it falls with the square of the length: the hip went 46.9 MPa at 4 mm to 8.2 at 10, the knee 25.4 to 9.1 at 7. Past about 10 MPa PETG cold flows under a standing load and the joint grows play — a leg that wobbles before anything has actually broken, which is the failure that does not look like one.

The arm's real load case is torsion. It runs along Y, so a fore/aft scuff at the foot puts that moment along its own axis: it does not bend, it twists, and an open rectangle is poor in torsion. At 9 × 14 it was safety factor 1.4 — the weakest thing on the robot while the bending table said the thigh was.

And the member carrying it was not the one being measured. The arm is two pieces at right angles: the long run goes along Y, the TONGUE goes along X out of the boss, and the tongue is the rooted end that carries everything. Its section is the BAND it crosses in, not the rail's width — and that band started 12 mm off the axis to clear the roll servo, a real constraint where the arm crosses the axis but not where the tongue is: the servo's body ends 7 mm out and the tongue starts at 11. Nothing was there. Running it to the axis and 12 mm past took the tongue from 10 × 22 to 34 × 24, and twisting it from SF 2.7 to 21.5. It also put all four horn screws in solid material landing on one flat face, where three of four had been breaking out through a curved surface.

Three parts are handed. The assembly mirrors each leg in Y for the right-hand side, so a part serves both sides only if it has a mirror plane of its own. Intersecting each with its own reflection: chassis, roof, pushrod and filler are symmetric in Y and the shoulder in Z, so one print each. Thigh, shank and crank have none and are 2 + 2. The shank comes out 99.0 % self-overlapping and the crank 95.2 %, which is the trap — they look symmetric. The shank's rocker lug sits at LINK_PHI off the beam and the crank's hub is a D-flat cut by the rod's sweep, and neither survives a reflection.

The chassis comes apart, because 280 mm does not fit on a 235 mm bed. Rotating does not help: for a rectangle on a rectangular bed, axis-aligned is already the best fit. It is a halved lap at the waist — 167 forward, 143 aft — six M3 through both, and two printed arrowhead latches that click it home and hold it square while you get the bolts in. Bending across the joint runs through 4 mm rather than 8, so 2.6 MPa becomes 10.4: safety factor 2.9. The latches are for assembly, not for strength — PETG creeps, and a snap left to carry the spine's bending on every step relaxes and lets go. The bolts are the joint.

Every joint has a fastener, and they are not all screws. Servo horns bolt to the part they drive with four M3 each, landing 1.5 mm into the horn's 2 — the horn is the only thread in that joint and the servo's output boss is immediately behind it, so the usable window is exactly 2 mm wide and the three lengths differ (M3×40, 16 and 20). The linkage pivots are shoulder bolts: a plain 4 mm shank longer than the rod is thick, so the rod turns on the shank while the thread pulls into its insert and the clamp load never reaches the rod. Nothing threads into bare plastic anywhere — 56 M3 and 4 M4 brass heat-set inserts take every thread, and the parts' pilot holes are drawn for a 4.6 mm OD insert, not a 4.0.

Interference is checked pairwise over 180 cases — four legs by forty-five poses, three hip angles by five knee angles by roll at −35, 0 and +35°. Not just at standing, and not just on one leg. Checking only the front-left proved nothing about the other three and three real clashes were hiding there; leaving roll pinned at zero hid two more. 46 pairs × 180 cases, all clear.

Servo bodies are drawn to DS3225MG dimensions. The 49.5 mm ear spacing is confirmed against its drawing; the horn's own bolt circle is assumed at 15 mm and is the one figure still worth calipers before you commit to twelve.
